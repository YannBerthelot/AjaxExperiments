\begin{abstract}
% ============================================================

Many continuous-control settings come equipped with a queryable expert
policy: a hand-engineered controller, a motion planner, a pattern
generator, or a behaviorally-cloned demonstrator. We study whether such
an expert can be used to train an RL policy that exceeds its own
performance, rather than merely match it. We identify a \emph{critic blind spot} as the
mechanistic failure of deterministic expert-selection schemes such as
IBRL: when the expert is always selected at states where it marginally
dominates in Q-value, the critic never observes the policy's own action
at those states and its estimate of $Q(s, \pi_\theta(s))$ stays
miscalibrated, which in turn corrupts the actor update that reads from
it. We propose \textbf{Expert-Decay-Guided Exploration (EDGE)}, a minimal
modification to any off-policy RL algorithm that mixes expert and policy
actions with a stochastic $\varepsilon$-greedy gate under a decaying
influence schedule, so every visited state contributes on-policy buffer
coverage with probability $1-p_t$. EDGE retains the buffer-coverage
benefit of expert guidance early in training while leaving the policy
free to surpass the expert asymptotically. We evaluate on five tasks
spanning setpoint tracking with PID experts (Plane3DCircle,
NuclearReactor, FourTank) and locomotion with CPG experts (CheetahRun,
BarkourJoystick), with 100 seeds per final configuration. We
pre-register the evaluation protocol, an ablation of each EDGE design
choice including IBRL's bootstrap component, a sensitivity analysis
across six expert-quality checkpoints, and three direct tests of the
buffer-coverage claim including critic calibration error on held-out
states.

\end{abstract}

% ============================================================
\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Many continuous-control problems come equipped with a queryable expert
policy: a hand-engineered feedback controller (PID, LQR, MPC), a motion
planner, a pattern generator, or a behaviorally-cloned demonstrator.
Such experts are rarely optimal but typically far better than a random
policy, and they encode knowledge that would be expensive to rediscover
from scratch. Most expert-guided RL papers (DAgger
\citep{ross2011dagger}, AWAC \citep{nair2020awac}, IBRL
\citep{hu2023ibrl}, RLPD \citep{ball2023rlpd}) treat the expert as an
upper bound: the goal is to match it. We study the complementary
question: given a controller already running on the plant, can we train
an RL policy that \emph{exceeds} its performance? The concrete
criterion throughout the paper is that a method must beat both the
RL-only baseline and the expert controller simultaneously.

\textbf{Contributions.}
(i) We identify a \emph{critic blind spot} induced by IBRL-style argmax
selection: by storing expert actions at every state where the expert's
Q-value marginally dominates, such schemes starve the critic of
observations of $Q(s, \pi_\theta(s))$ at those states, leaving the
actor update miscalibrated. We propose \textbf{EDGE}, a minimal
$\varepsilon$-greedy expert mixing method under a decay schedule that
guarantees every visited state appears in the buffer under the current
policy with probability $1-p_t$.
(ii) We evaluate EDGE across five tasks spanning two families
(Plane3DCircle, NuclearReactor, FourTank with PID experts; CheetahRun,
BarkourJoystick with CPG experts), at 100 seeds per final configuration,
against IBRL, JSRL, Residual SAC, and SAC.
(iii) We pre-register (a) an ablation of each EDGE design choice
including IBRL's bootstrap as a separable ingredient, (b) a sensitivity
analysis to expert degradation via parameter perturbation, and (c)
direct tests of the buffer-coverage claim including critic calibration
error, turning Proposition~\ref{prop:blindspot} from theoretical into
falsifiable.

\section{Problem Formalization}
\label{sec:problem}
% ============================================================

We consider a continuous-control MDP
$(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, T, R, \gamma)$ with state space
$\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, action space $\mathcal{A} = [-1,1]^d$,
transition function $T: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{S}$, and
reward $R: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \to \mathbb{R}$. Alongside the MDP
we are given a deterministic \emph{expert controller} $\pi^* : \mathcal{S}
\to \mathcal{A}$, typically a hand-engineered PID, LQR, MPC, or pattern
generator, that can be queried at any state visited during training. The
expert is not assumed to be optimal: we evaluate against it as a floor,
not as a target.

The agent's objective is the standard
$J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t R(s_t, a_t)\right]$.
We denote the expert's return as $J_{\text{exp}}$, the RL-only baseline's
return at convergence as $J_{\text{RL}}$, and the empirical upper bound
reached by a large-budget RL agent as $J^*$. Our evaluation criterion is
that a candidate method must \emph{simultaneously} exceed both
$J_{\text{exp}}$ and $J_{\text{RL}}$ on every task where either baseline
leaves measurable headroom.

\paragraph{Expert-mixed behavior policy.}
EDGE acts under an expert-mixed behavior policy: at each step, the executed
action is sampled from
\begin{equation}
    \beta_t(a\mid s) \;=\; p_t\,\delta_{\pi^*(s)}(a)\;+\;(1-p_t)\,\pi_\theta(a\mid s),
    \label{eq:behavior}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_\theta$ is the current learner and $p_t \in [0,1]$ is a
non-increasing mixing schedule with $p_0 \leq \varepsilon$ and
$p_t \to 0$. The replay buffer stores $(s, a, r, s')$ tuples with $a \sim
\beta_t$, and is consumed by any off-policy RL algorithm that tolerates
arbitrary full-support behavior policies (e.g.\ SAC). The expert-selection
event at each step is independent of the state, so the Bernoulli gate and
the state distribution decouple in all subsequent analyses.

\begin{remark}[Asymptotic consistency]
\label{rem:asymptotic}
If the base off-policy algorithm $A$ converges to a behavior-policy
independent stationary point $\pi^{A}_\infty$ under any full-support
behavior policy, and $p_t \to 0$, then EDGE inherits that limit: from the
first step with $p_t = 0$ onwards EDGE is operationally identical to $A$
run on its own, and modifying a finite prefix of the replay stream does
not alter the fixed point under standard stochastic-approximation
assumptions. We do not claim a convergence rate.
\end{remark}

We state this as a remark rather than a theorem because its content is a
sanity check on the decay schedule (the expert leaves no footprint on the
asymptotic fixed point) rather than an independent result. A rate-style
statement tying the decay horizon to critic calibration speed is an open
question we return to in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

% ============================================================
\section{Related Work}
% ============================================================

\textbf{Imitation-regime methods.} DAgger \citep{ross2011dagger} mixes expert
and policy actions on a fixed decay schedule but stores the expert action
as a supervised label, so the learner cannot exceed the expert. EDGE
reuses the same $\varepsilon$-greedy gate but stores the executed action
with its reward and next-state, making the collected transitions valid for
off-policy RL rather than only for behavior cloning. AWAC
\citep{nair2020awac} adds an advantage-weighted likelihood penalty pulling
the actor toward high-advantage offline actions, a policy-space
intervention distinct from EDGE's data-collection intervention.

\textbf{Expert-rollin methods.} JSRL \citep{uchendu2023jsrl} hands control
from the expert to the learner at a scheduled switch step, producing a
hard state-visitation curriculum; EDGE uses a per-step Bernoulli gate
instead, so both expert-style and policy-style transitions are collected
at every stage of training and the policy is never starved of its own
on-policy coverage. Policy Expansion \citep{zhang2023pex} keeps a frozen
offline-pretrained policy alongside the learner and selects between them
by Q-value at execution time, essentially a soft-argmax variant of IBRL
and subject to the same critic-coverage concern we analyze in
Section~\ref{sec:method}. LOGO \citep{rengarajan2022logo} adds a KL
penalty to a behavioral prior, a policy-space intervention that is
orthogonal to EDGE's data-collection intervention.

\textbf{Expert-at-training methods.} IBRL \citep{hu2023ibrl} is the closest
prior work: it trains an IL policy and selects between IL and RL actions by
argmax on Q-values, and additionally uses the IL policy for Bellman-target
bootstrapping. EDGE differs by (i) stochastic $\varepsilon$-greedy mixing
instead of argmax (avoiding the critic blind spot of
Section~\ref{sec:method}), (ii) a closed-form expert (no IL pretraining),
and (iii) no bootstrap proposal. RLPD \citep{ball2023rlpd} oversamples a
static demonstration buffer at 50\% of every batch with high UTD,
LayerNorm, and Q-ensembling; EDGE instead generates fresh expert
transitions at the states the current policy actually visits. Residual RL
\citep{johannink2019residual} adds the policy output to the expert action,
which imposes a structural ceiling at $a_{\text{exp}} \pm \delta$; EDGE
eventually acts independently.

\textbf{Offline-to-online methods.} IQL \citep{kostrikov2022iql} and
similar offline-RL approaches followed by online fine-tuning are an
alternative way to use an existing controller: roll the controller out
once to collect a static dataset, pretrain offline, then fine-tune. This
axis is complementary to ours (static-dataset vs.\ queryable-expert), and
we treat RLPD as the representative of that family here because it is the
most competitive online algorithm built from static expert data.

\textbf{Uncertainty-driven exploration.} SUNRISE \citep{lee2021sunrise} uses
UCB exploration via critic-ensemble uncertainty; it has no expert and
cannot leverage external policy knowledge, but the ensemble-uncertainty
signal is the same ingredient we discuss for an uncertainty-adaptive
variant of EDGE in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

In summary, EDGE inherits DAgger's stochastic decaying gate but stores
the executed action rather than an expert label, and inherits IBRL's
buffer-enrichment role but replaces argmax with a Bernoulli gate. JSRL
shares the decaying-expert-rollin role but uses a hard step-indexed
handoff instead of a per-step stochastic gate. Residual RL combines
expert and policy additively rather than by selection.

\section{Expert-Decay-Guided Exploration (EDGE)}
\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Critic Blind Spots Motivate Stochastic Mixing}

Before presenting the algorithm we make explicit the failure mode that
motivates it. IBRL-style deterministic selection takes the argmax over
$\{Q_\phi(s, \pi^*(s)), Q_\phi(s, \pi_\theta(s))\}$ and executes the
winner. Whenever the expert wins at state $s$, the transition stored in
the replay buffer is $(s, \pi^*(s), r, s')$, and the critic's Bellman
regression target on that tuple is an estimate of $Q(s, \pi^*(s))$, not
of $Q(s, \pi_\theta(s))$. The SAC actor update reads $\nabla_\theta
\mathbb{E}_{s \sim \mathcal{B}, a \sim \pi_\theta}[Q_\phi(s, a) - \alpha
\log \pi_\theta(a|s)]$; the stored action does not enter it directly, but
its correctness depends entirely on $Q_\phi(s, \pi_\theta(s))$ being
calibrated. Under persistent argmax selection that quantity is never
anchored by a Bellman update, so the actor optimizes against a
miscalibrated target. We refer to this as a \emph{critic blind spot}.

\begin{proposition}[Critic blind spot under argmax selection]
\label{prop:blindspot}
Fix a training step $t$ and define
\[
    \mathcal{M}_t \;=\; \{s : Q_\phi(s,\pi^*(s)) > Q_\phi(s,\pi_\theta(s))\}.
\]
Under IBRL-style argmax selection without decay, the replay buffer
contains no transition of the form $(s, a, \cdot, \cdot)$ with $s \in
\mathcal{M}_t$ and $a \neq \pi^*(s)$. The Bellman regression loss
$\sum_{(s,a,r,s') \in \mathcal{B}} (Q_\phi(s,a) - r - \gamma
Q_{\bar\phi}(s', \cdot))^2$ is therefore insensitive, at every $s \in
\mathcal{M}_t$, to the value of $Q_\phi$ at inputs $(s, a)$ with $a
\neq \pi^*(s)$. Consequently $Q_\phi(s, \pi_\theta(s))$ at
$s \in \mathcal{M}_t$ is determined solely by function-approximator
generalization from neighbouring state-action pairs. The actor update
reads $\nabla_\theta Q_\phi(s, \pi_\theta(s))$ as its improvement
signal at $s$ despite $Q_\phi(s, \pi_\theta(s))$ receiving no TD
correction. Whenever $\mathcal{M}_t$ remains non-empty across a
positive fraction of training steps, the bias is persistent.
\end{proposition}

The failure is mechanistic, not statistical: even with unlimited
environment data, pure argmax never records a transition whose Bellman
target updates $Q_\phi(s, \pi_\theta(s))$ at $s \in \mathcal{M}_t$. The
positive counterpart follows immediately from the Bernoulli-gate
decoupling of \eqref{eq:behavior}.

\begin{lemma}[Critic coverage under $\varepsilon$-mixing]
\label{lem:coverage}
Let $\beta_t$ be the expert-mixed behavior policy \eqref{eq:behavior}, and
let $\mu_t$ denote the induced state visitation distribution at step $t$.
Then for every $s$ with $\mu_t(s) > 0$, the probability that the
transition stored at $s$ has action drawn from $\pi_\theta$, and
therefore provides a Bellman target anchoring $Q_\phi(s,
\pi_\theta(s))$, is exactly $1 - p_t$. In particular, as long as $p_t <
1$, every visited state accumulates policy-action critic anchors at
expected rate $(1-p_t)\,\mu_t(s)$, so no persistent blind set analogous
to $\mathcal{M}_t$ can form under $\beta_t$.
\end{lemma}

The proof is a one-line Bernoulli argument: the expert-selection event
at each step is independent of the state by construction of $\beta_t$.
EDGE therefore avoids the blind spot of
Proposition~\ref{prop:blindspot} by design. We revisit the same effect
from a buffer-diversity perspective in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Algorithm}

EDGE is a minimal modification to any off-policy RL algorithm. Relative
to DAgger, the only algorithmic change is that the replay tuple records
the executed action together with its realized reward and next state,
rather than the expert's label under the executed state: this single
change turns the collected data from a behavior-cloning dataset into a
valid off-policy RL dataset, and is what lets the learner eventually
exceed the expert. At each environment step, a Bernoulli gate determines
whether the expert or the policy acts:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Expert-Decay-Guided Exploration (EDGE)}
\label{alg:ege}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Hyperparameters:} $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$, $T_{\text{decay}}$
\STATE Initialize base off-policy algorithm (policy $\pi_\theta$, critic
       $Q_\phi$, replay buffer $\mathcal{B}$)
\FOR{step $t = 1, \ldots, T$}
    \STATE Observe state $s_t$
    \STATE Compute gate probability:
    \[
        p_t = \varepsilon \cdot \max\!\left(1 - \frac{t}{T_{\text{decay}}}, 0\right)
    \]
    \IF{$\text{Bernoulli}(p_t) = 1$}
        \STATE $a_t \leftarrow \pi^*(s_t)$ \hfill (expert action)
    \ELSE
        \STATE $a_t \leftarrow \pi_\theta(s_t)$ \hfill (policy action)
    \ENDIF
    \STATE Execute $a_t$, observe $r_t$, $s_{t+1}$
    \STATE Store $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ in $\mathcal{B}$ \hfill
           \textit{(executed action, not labeled)}
    \STATE Update $\pi_\theta$, $Q_\phi$ via the base off-policy algorithm
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Key design choices.} EDGE makes five design choices, each
validated by an ablation (Sections~\ref{sec:ablation} and
\ref{sec:exploration}): \textit{(i) use an expert at all}, contrasted
with SAC and with a random-action source at matched rate; \textit{(ii)
stochastic gating}, a state-independent Bernoulli gate rather than an
argmax, contrasted with IBRL-style argmax at matched expert rate to
test Proposition~\ref{prop:blindspot}; \textit{(iii) decaying gate
probability}, contrasted with a constant gate; \textit{(iv) store the
executed action} (expert or policy, whichever was taken) with its
realized reward and next state, as opposed to DAgger-style storage of
the expert's label regardless of what was executed, or storage of the
learner's action regardless of what was executed, either of which
would break the off-policy validity of the collected data; \textit{(v)
no bootstrap proposal}, i.e.\ the expert enriches the buffer but is
not used in Bellman target computation, contrasted with IBRL's full
bootstrap variant.

EDGE adds two hyperparameters on top of the base algorithm: mixing
probability $\varepsilon$ (default $0.5$) and decay horizon
$T_{\text{decay}}$ (default $0.15\,T_{\text{total}}$). Both defaults
are swept in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}. When instantiated with SAC as
the base algorithm, as in all our experiments, we refer to the method
as EDGE-SAC.

\section{Experiments}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Methodology principle.} Every design choice in EDGE is
required to clear two bars: a stated motivation (theoretical argument
or explicit intuition) and an experimental validation against a
plausible alternative. This applies to the use of an expert at all
(vs.\ random-action source, Section~\ref{sec:exploration}), the
stochastic gate (vs.\ argmax at matched rate), the decay schedule, the
default mixing probability, and the absence of expert-action
bootstrapping, each ablated in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.

\subsection{Setup}

\subsubsection{Benchmark Tasks}

We evaluate on five tasks spanning two families. The setpoint-tracking
family uses PID experts on \textbf{Plane3DCircle} (3D aircraft circular
trajectory tracking, \texttt{target\_gym}), \textbf{NuclearReactor}
(point-kinetics reactor load-following, 24\,h episodes, physics at
$\Delta t{=}1$\,s with a 10\,s control period), and \textbf{FourTank}
(the quadruple-tank MIMO process of
\citet{johansson2000quadruple}, non-minimum-phase). The locomotion
family uses Central Pattern Generator (CPG) experts on
\textbf{CheetahRun} (dm\_control cheetah via Mujoco Playground
\citep{mujocoplayground2024}) and \textbf{BarkourJoystick} (quadruped
joystick-velocity task, also Mujoco Playground). The three setpoint
tasks stress different failure modes of PID control: FourTank is the
only MIMO-coupled task (a per-loop PID cannot decouple); NuclearReactor
is the only long-horizon task with stiff dynamics (xenon transients on
24\,h timescales); Plane3DCircle is the only 3D kinematic-tracking task
with a continuously rotating reference direction. Full task
descriptions, observation/action spaces, and reward forms are in
Appendix~\ref{app:tasks}.
Each task comes with a reliable, reproducibly tuned expert: PID gains from
relay autotuning under AMIGO rules \citep{astrom1984relay}, CPG parameters
from differential evolution on episodic return (details in
Appendix~\ref{app:tuning}). Measured expert performance per task is
reported in Appendix~\ref{app:tasks}, Table~\ref{tab:expert_headroom}.

\subsubsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

All methods are tuned by a common HPO protocol; the exact budget and
sampler settings are in Appendix~\ref{app:hpo}. For consistency across
the comparison, every expert-using baseline is implemented on top of
SAC as the base off-policy algorithm, including methods that were
originally proposed for a different base algorithm (e.g.\ IBRL was
proposed on top of TD3). This holds the base learner fixed across the
comparison so the remaining differences are attributable to the
expert-integration mechanism, not to the choice of off-policy
algorithm. The baselines are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SAC} \citep{haarnoja2018sac}, the primary RL baseline.
    \item \textbf{Expert controller}: the tuned PID or CPG, no RL. Primary
    controller baseline.
    \item \textbf{IBRL} \citep{hu2023ibrl}, the full method: argmax
    action selection over $\{Q_\phi(s, \pi^*(s)), Q_\phi(s,
    \pi_\theta(s))\}$ together with expert-action bootstrapping in the
    Bellman target, using our closed-form expert in place of IBRL's
    IL-pretrained policy. Tests \emph{argmax vs.\ stochastic} gating
    against the strongest IBRL configuration. Removal of the bootstrap
    component is reported as an ablation in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.
    \item \textbf{JSRL} \citep{uchendu2023jsrl}, hard step-indexed handoff:
    expert rolls in for the first $H$ steps per episode, policy acts
    thereafter, with $H$ decayed over training. Tests \emph{hard handoff
    vs.\ Bernoulli} gating.
    \item \textbf{Residual SAC} \citep{johannink2019residual}, with
    $a = \mathrm{clip}(a_{\text{exp}} + a_{\text{res}}, -1, 1)$. Tests
    \emph{additive structural combination vs.\ mixing}.
    \item \textbf{EDGE (ours)}: $\varepsilon$-greedy expert mixing with
    decay, $\varepsilon{=}0.5$, horizon $0.15\,T_{\text{total}}$.
\end{itemize}

Two further expert-using methods do not fit the axis this comparison
isolates and are reported in the appendix rather than the main table.
RLPD \citep{ball2023rlpd} targets the static-demo-buffer setting rather
than the queryable-expert setting we study; we report a best-effort
adaptation (50\% expert transitions per batch, high UTD, LayerNorm) in
Appendix~\ref{app:rlpd} as a sanity check. A \emph{Parameterized Expert
Policy} baseline, in which the RL actor modulates the expert's own
parameters ($p_i = p_i^{\text{anchor}}\cdot\exp(\log(10)\cdot a_i)$) on
a log scale, tests a qualitatively different design (parameter-level
vs.\ full-action control) and is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:param}.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Protocol}

All final experiments use 100 seeds per configuration; HPO uses 20 seeds per
trial. Metrics reported:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IQM of steps to $\Delta = 0$}: median number of steps for a
          seed to first exceed the expert. Handles non-crossing seeds via
          right-censoring at $T + 1$.
    \item \textbf{IQM of steps to $\Delta = 250$}: same, at sustained
          superiority. (Threshold adapted per task.)
    \item \textbf{Final expert advantage}: IQM of $\Delta$ averaged over the
          last 200k steps.
    \item \textbf{Fraction crossing}: fraction of seeds that exceed the expert
          at any point during training.
    \item \textbf{Normalized return} $\tilde{J} = (J - J_{\text{exp}}) /
          (J^* - J_{\text{exp}})$: the fraction of remaining headroom captured.
          Meaningful across tasks with different absolute reward scales.
\end{itemize}

Statistical tests: AUC permutation test, Mann-Whitney U on crossing times,
Welch's t-test on final performance. All tests Bonferroni-corrected at
$\alpha = 0.05$.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

We ablate EDGE's load-bearing design choices on two reference tasks,
FourTank (cheap, low-variance MIMO) and CheetahRun (locomotion,
non-setpoint), at 100 seeds per condition and an extended $2\times$ HPO
budget. [Results pending.]

\textbf{Gating style (central).} Fixed-$\varepsilon$ stochastic mixing
vs.\ IBRL-style argmax, matched marginal expert rate and decay. Argmax
underperformance is direct evidence for the blind-spot mechanism.

\textbf{IBRL bootstrap proposal.} Separates argmax action selection
from expert-action bootstrapping in the Bellman target (the two
mechanisms IBRL composes).

\textbf{Decay horizon.} $T_{\text{decay}} \in \{0.05, 0.15, 0.50, 1.00,
\infty\} \cdot T_{\text{total}}$. Pre-registered: $0.15$ minimizes
steps-to-$\Delta{=}0$; $\infty$ maximizes asymptote at slower
convergence.

\textbf{Mixing probability.} $\varepsilon \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75,
0.9\}$. Pre-registered: plateau across $[0.3, 0.7]$; too low collapses
to SAC, too high starves the critic of policy-action samples and
reintroduces the blind spot.

\subsection{Probing the Exploration Claim}
\label{sec:exploration}

The abstract and Section~\ref{sec:method} frame EDGE's advantage as a
buffer-coverage effect: the expert populates regions of state space the
policy would otherwise reach only slowly, and the stochastic gate
ensures the critic is anchored at policy actions at those same states.
We pre-register three experiments that turn this framing into measurable
predictions. All run on Plane3DCircle, 100 seeds per condition.

\textbf{Random-mix baseline.} EDGE with the expert replaced by a
uniform random draw from $[-1,1]^d$ at the same
$\varepsilon$-schedule, isolating expert \emph{action quality} from
behavior-policy \emph{stochasticity}. If EDGE $\gg$ random-mix, the
expert's specific actions carry the signal; if EDGE $\approx$
random-mix, the benefit is action-level noise that any stochastic
policy would provide.

\textbf{Replay-buffer state-visitation coverage.} At fixed training
checkpoints, score the buffer against a reference set $\mathcal{R}$ of
high-value states from a long expert rollout, reporting mean $k$-NN
distance and fraction-of-$\mathcal{R}$-within-radius. Pre-registered
hypothesis: EDGE reaches a fixed coverage level strictly earlier than
SAC and no later than IBRL; failure falsifies the buffer-coverage
framing.

\textbf{Critic calibration error (direct test of
Proposition~\ref{prop:blindspot}).} On held-out states
$\mathcal{S}_{\text{eval}}$, estimate $Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,
\pi_\theta(s))$ by Monte-Carlo rollout and compare to $Q_\phi(s,
\pi_\theta(s))$; report $\ell_2$ calibration error over training for
IBRL vs.\ EDGE, split by $s \in \mathcal{M}_t$ vs.\ $s \notin
\mathcal{M}_t$. Pre-registered hypothesis: IBRL's error on
$\mathcal{M}_t$ stays above EDGE's throughout training while the two
match off $\mathcal{M}_t$. Failure would indicate that
Proposition~\ref{prop:blindspot}, though correct as a statement about
the Bellman anchor, is not the dominant practical mechanism. [Results
pending.]

\subsection{Sensitivity to Expert Sub-Optimality}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

Real industrial controllers are not perfectly optimal, so the most
practically consequential question about EDGE is how gracefully it
degrades as the expert weakens. We pre-register two degradation axes and
report, for each, the crossover point at which the degraded-expert
EDGE run stops outperforming vanilla SAC.

\textbf{Checkpoint degradation (primary).} The expert is replaced with a
SAC checkpoint frozen at $\{25\%, 50\%, 75\%, 90\%, 95\%, 100\%\}$ of the
empirical upper bound $J^*$. Each checkpoint is a fully self-consistent
expert (same policy used for buffer enrichment and action selection), so
there is no action-label mismatch. Run on Plane3DCircle and FourTank
(both have clear, well-separated headroom to $J^*$), 100 seeds per
cell.

\textbf{Action-noise degradation (secondary).} Expert actions perturbed
as $a_{\text{deg}} = \mathrm{clip}(a^\star + \sigma \cdot
\mathcal{N}(0,I), -1, 1)$ for $\sigma \in \{0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.50\}$.
This isolates the effect of action-level stochasticity from the
policy-quality effect captured above.

\textbf{Reported metrics.} For each (task, degradation-level) cell we
report (a) normalized return $\tilde{J}$ of EDGE relative to the same
task's SAC baseline, (b) the fraction of seeds that still exceed
$J_{\text{exp}}$ at end of training, and (c) a paired sign-test between
EDGE and SAC across seeds. We define the \emph{minimum expert quality}
$q^\star$ as the smallest checkpoint level at which EDGE's IQM final
return remains above SAC's with Bonferroni-corrected $p < 0.05$.

\textbf{What the result means for practitioners.} If $q^\star$ is well
below $100\%$ (expected), the number directly answers the field question
``how tuned does my controller have to be before EDGE is worth
deploying?'' If $q^\star$ approaches $100\%$, EDGE's utility is narrow
and the paper's framing weakens; we preregister this outcome and will
report it either way. [Results pending.]

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Why Stochastic Mixing Works}

The superiority of stochastic $\varepsilon$-greedy over argmax can be
understood through two complementary lenses:

\textbf{Buffer diversity.} Fixed-$\varepsilon$ produces a replay buffer
that is a stochastic mixture of expert and policy transitions at every
visited state. The critic receives Bellman anchors for both action
distributions simultaneously. Argmax produces a near-deterministic
buffer: at every state where the expert currently dominates, only
expert-action transitions are stored. The critic learns that expert
actions are good but never receives a Bellman anchor for policy actions
at those states, which is the blind spot of
Proposition~\ref{prop:blindspot}.

\textbf{Downstream actor-update quality.} Although the SAC actor update
does not read the stored action directly, it reads $Q_\phi(s,
\pi_\theta(s))$, which in the argmax case is unanchored at $s \in
\mathcal{M}_t$ and determined by generalization from neighbouring
state-action pairs. The actor therefore climbs a miscalibrated surface
at exactly the states where the expert is competitive, which is the
opposite of the regime where fine-grained improvement matters.

\subsection{The Role of the Expert}

Our results reveal an interesting asymmetry: the expert is not primarily
useful because its actions are better (RL eventually exceeds the expert on
every task in our benchmark where the expert leaves headroom). It is useful
because its transitions populate regions of the replay buffer that the
policy's own exploration would reach only slowly, high-reward states on
setpoint-tracking tasks, and coherent gait-cycle trajectories on locomotion
tasks. This is a data-distribution effect, not a policy-quality effect.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Expert availability.} EDGE requires a queryable expert at every
state visited during training. For experts that are a controller, a
planner, or a pretrained policy running alongside the agent, this is
typically satisfied. For settings where the expert can only be queried
on a fixed dataset (e.g.\ human demonstrations without an underlying
policy), a buffer-only approach is necessary.

\textbf{Reward-structure dependence.} Our tasks use dense, shaped
rewards. In sparse-reward regimes, the expert's role would shift from
``providing high-value states'' to ``providing any non-zero reward
signal,'' which changes the theoretical analysis. We leave sparse-reward
extensions to future work.

\textbf{Scope.} Evaluation is in simulation with queryable deterministic
experts on continuous-control tasks; we make no sim-to-real claims.
Extension to discrete actions or stochastic experts (replace
$\delta_{\pi^*(s)}$ with the expert action distribution) is
straightforward in principle but untested. Three of five tasks are
PID-regulated setpoint-tracking problems; while they span distinct
failure modes of PID control (MIMO coupling, long-horizon stiffness, 3D
kinematic tracking), high-dimensional manipulation and image-based
observations are not covered.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We study expert-guided RL under the concrete practitioner criterion of
beating both the RL-only baseline and the expert-controller baseline
simultaneously, across setpoint tracking tasks with PID experts and
locomotion tasks with CPG experts. We identify a \emph{critic blind
spot} as the specific failure of IBRL-style argmax selection: persistent
expert-action storage at states in $\mathcal{M}_t$ leaves $Q_\phi(s,
\pi_\theta(s))$ unanchored by any Bellman update, corrupting the target
the SAC actor reads. We propose \textbf{EDGE}, a minimal
$\varepsilon$-greedy expert mixing method that avoids this failure mode
by construction, and pre-register an evaluation and ablation protocol
targeting the buffer-coverage claim directly, including critic
calibration error on held-out states.

The method targets the practical question of how to leverage an
already-tuned controller to produce an RL policy that outperforms it,
rather than merely reproduce it. For practitioners deploying RL
alongside existing controllers (industrial PIDs, locomotion pattern
generators, or any other hand-engineered feedback law), EDGE is a
drop-in modification to standard off-policy RL that converts the
controller from a target to a scaffold: it seeds the replay buffer with
high-value transitions during an initial decaying-influence phase, then
releases the policy to exceed it.

\paragraph{Future work.}

Several directions follow naturally. First, the fixed decay schedule is
a practical compromise; a principled alternative is to tie decay to
critic-ensemble disagreement, giving an \emph{uncertainty-adaptive}
variant (UA-EDGE) in which the transition from exploration-like to
exploitation-like gating is driven by measured critic calibration rather
than a fixed schedule.

Second, the benchmark currently covers setpoint tracking (PID experts) and
locomotion (CPG experts). Natural additions are manipulation with a
PD-to-target expert (Mujoco Playground's Reacher family) and process-control
tasks with MPC experts. Each addition should come with its own principled
tuning protocol, following Appendix~\ref{app:tuning}.

Third, we conjecture the critic blind spot admits a tight bound on the
calibration error on $\mathcal{M}_t$ in terms of the Lipschitz constant
of $Q^{\pi_\theta}$ and the local coverage radius, yielding a
sample-complexity comparison between EDGE and IBRL. A formal analysis
is left for future work.

\section{Hyperparameter Optimization Protocol}
\label{app:hpo}
% ============================================================

All baselines are tuned by a common protocol to ensure a fair comparison.
We use Optuna's TPE sampler with the \texttt{constant\_liar}/\texttt{multivariate}
options enabled and an asynchronous median pruner. The per-method search
budget is 30 trials; each trial trains 20 seeds for $10^6$ environment
steps and reports the interquartile mean of final-window returns. Trials
are pruned once their trailing mean falls below the median of completed
trials at the same training step, with a warmup of 300k steps. The search
space per method mirrors SAC's (learning rates, discount, target
smoothing, entropy target, batch/arch size); EDGE adds four method-specific
dimensions (expert mixing probability, decay horizon, fixed-probability
tail, policy-update start). Final evaluation uses 100 freshly-seeded
training runs per configuration at the best-found hyperparameters.

\section{Expert Tuning Protocols}
\label{app:tuning}
% ============================================================

The main body treats each task's expert as a black-box oracle. This appendix
describes how each expert is actually tuned, why we chose these protocols,
and what tuning would have been unfair.

\subsection{PID Experts, Relay Autotuning with Operating-Point Sweep}

We follow the Åström--Hägglund relay-feedback procedure
\citep{astrom1984relay}: inject a bang-bang relay on the actuator, measure
the sustained-oscillation ultimate gain $K_u$ and period $T_u$, and map to
PID gains via the AMIGO tuning rules \citep{astrom2005amigo}. For each
tracked loop we sweep $N=8$ operating points uniformly across the setpoint
range and retain the middle-point gains, mirroring gain-scheduled industrial
practice. MIMO altitude loops are tuned as two sequential SISO loops
(pump-to-altitude and stick-to-altitude), each relay-tuned against the same
altitude error, following the \texttt{target\_gym} quadruple-tank
convention. Cascade loops (aircraft bank-inside-heading) are tuned
inside-out: inner bank loop first, then outer heading loop with bank closed.
All gains are stored in a versioned JSON file to support reproduction and
per-seed ablation.

For the nuclear reactor, physics are integrated at $\Delta t = 1$\,s for
numerical stability while the PID operates every 10 physics steps (10\,s
control period); the relay experiment is run at the control rate, so $T_u$
is reported in control steps.

\subsection{CPG Experts, Differential Evolution on Episodic Return}

CPG parameters are tuned by differential evolution \citep{storn1997de} on
the mean per-seed episodic return, using SciPy's
\texttt{differential\_evolution} with population multiplier $5$, $30$
iterations, and 8-seed evaluation per candidate. Search bounds: frequency
$\in [0.5, 5.0]$ Hz, amplitudes $\in [0, 1]$ per actuator, phases $\in
[0, 2\pi]$ per actuator. On CheetahRun this protocol improves the CPG's mean
return from $91.5$ (hand-tuned) to $221.8$ (DE-tuned), a $2.4\times$
improvement at fixed structure. The CheetahRun DE search converged by
iteration $\approx 22$ of 30 in all replicates.

\subsection{Why These Protocols}

Expert tuning is a hidden confound in expert-guided RL comparisons. A
poorly-tuned expert inflates the headroom available to the RL agent, and
an over-tuned expert (one using reward information that the baseline should
not have) would leak the RL problem into the baseline. Relay autotuning
uses only physical plant identification (no reward access); DE-tuning uses
return but at a fixed structural form (open-loop sinusoid) that cannot
trivially exceed the RL upper bound. Both protocols are reproducible from
the plant simulator alone.

\section{Parameterized Expert Policy}
\label{app:param}
% ============================================================

A natural alternative to mixing expert and policy \emph{actions} is to
keep the expert's structural form and let the RL actor tune its
parameters online. We report such a baseline here. The RL actor outputs a
log-scale modulation $p_i = p_i^{\text{anchor}} \cdot
\exp(\log(10)\cdot a_i)$ of each expert parameter: PID gains
$(K_p, K_i, K_d)$ per tracked loop for the setpoint-tracking tasks, and
frequency plus per-actuator amplitudes for the CPG-driven locomotion
tasks (phases are held fixed). The anchor values are the tuned
parameters from Appendix~\ref{app:tuning}. This is a qualitatively
different design from EDGE: the agent never produces a raw action, only
a re-parameterization of the expert, and cannot express behaviors
outside the expert's structural family. We report it for reference
rather than as a head-to-head baseline; full results pending.

\section{RLPD-Style Sanity Check}
\label{app:rlpd}
% ============================================================

RLPD \citep{ball2023rlpd} was designed for the setting where a fixed
offline demonstration buffer is available at the start of training and
sampled at 50\% of every batch alongside the online replay. Our setting
differs: the expert is queryable online, and there is no fixed offline
buffer to begin with. To still report a comparison, we run a
best-effort adaptation (``RLPD-style''): we continuously write expert
transitions into a dedicated demo buffer during training and enforce a
50\%/50\% sampling split between that buffer and the online buffer at
every critic update, with RLPD's high UTD and LayerNorm settings. This
adaptation is a confounded comparison in both directions (it gives RLPD
online expert-collection benefits it was not designed for, and the
50\% split is not tuned for our tasks) and we report it only as a
sanity check that the EDGE win is not trivially reproduced by a static-
demo-buffer template. [Results pending.]
